The $100k role · age 23

The chief of staff DM

The cold message that got a reply in thirty minutes, and the role it turned into. Reproduced exactly as it was sent, typos included, with notes on why each part is there.

1. Before I sent it

Alec was building RallyUp in public and posting about it. That post is the only reason the message exists: it told me what he was working on, what he needed, and that he was paying attention to his own feed that week.

I was working full time as a partnerships manager in Spain. I was not looking for a job title. I was looking for a founder who needed the one thing I already knew how to do.

I read his last twenty posts before I wrote a word. Not to flatter him back, to learn how he talks: which words he reaches for, what he finds interesting, how long his sentences run. Then I wrote to him in his own language, reusing his own phrases, because a message that sounds like you is a message you finish reading.

The posts also told me what was missing. Outreach was the obvious gap. It was not the only one: nothing was tracking who had been spoken to or what had been said. So I did not pitch one service. I pitched the thing he knew he needed, and the thing he had not got to yet.

2. The message

Sent 3:28pm

Hey Alec, Saw your post and just had to reach out, as the outreach hacker I am. "How did he find about me?", you're asking yourself. Well, easy. The same way I will find about your future clients to get you to +$100k per month. Now that I have your attention, I'm Francisco, 3 things about me: 1. Moved to the UK for University at 17, started my first business at 18, made $10k organising and selling event tickets to international students (money I dropped stupidly on a watch). 2. I ran LinkedIn outreach for Farhoon Asim, yeah, the 85k+ LinkedIn follower guy, pulling in multiple $100k months by selling in the DMs. 3. Now working as a Partnerships Manager at a high-growth SaaS in Spain, have some extra time on afternoons and weekends, and would love to help you sell more RallyUp to teams! If you want an outreach hacker who actually talks and sells like a human (not a robot), I'm your guy. Let's speak, you can set up a call here - [calendar link] Big hug from Spain, Francisco

3. Why each part is there

01
The opening names the trigger, not me. "Saw your post" tells him this was not sent to four hundred people. The self-label that follows does the introduction in four words so the next line can do the work.
02
The highlighted line is the entire pitch. It says the quiet part out loud: I found you the same way I will find your customers. It is not a claim about outreach, it is outreach, arriving in his inbox while he reads the claim. That is the only part of this message that cannot be copied by someone who cannot do the work.
03
The introduction comes fourth, not first. "Now that I have your attention" is only allowed because the line before it earned the attention. Leading with your name and your CV spends the attention you have not got yet.
04
Three facts, each carrying a number and none of them a job title. A business at eighteen with the amount attached. A named client with a follower count and a revenue figure. Current status with the honest admission of limited hours. The self-deprecating line about the watch is there so the first two do not read as boasting.
05
The positioning line attacks the category's worst habit. Everyone doing outreach sounds like a robot. Saying so, in a message that plainly does not, is a second demonstration.
06
A calendar link, not a question. No "let me know if this is of interest". The whole message assumes the call, so the last line just removes the friction.
07
It is not polished. There is a grammatical slip in the best line in the message. It went anyway, and it worked, because specificity beats polish every single time.

4. What came back

Replied 3:58pm, thirty minutes later

Great work. Booked for Wednesday.

That was the whole reply. I started fractionally, on afternoons and weekends around a full time job, which is exactly what the third fact in the message had offered.

The reply is not the interesting part though. What turned a few afternoons into a full time role was the first month: I shipped things before he asked for them, and built what was missing instead of waiting to be told it was missing. That is the difference between a freelancer somebody is paying and a person they would struggle to run the company without.

Grind beats talent. Every time.
