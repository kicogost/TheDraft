5 post formats · 30-day calendar

The AI LinkedIn plan

A LinkedIn plan for AI SaaS founders: five post formats that show how your product actually works, a template and a worked example for each, the weekly posting schedule and a 30-day calendar.

What this is

I'm a partner at RallyUp, where we write LinkedIn for B2B founders and executives. Our clients are backed by YC, a16z and more, and we've generated 21.3M+ impressions for the founders and execs we write for.

AI companies on LinkedIn all sound alike. Agentic, 10x your team, the future of work: your buyer has scrolled past some version of that line from dozens of vendors this month, and by now they can't tell any of you apart.

So every post in this plan shows the product doing its job on a problem your buyer already has, and you can skip the "AI is changing everything" posts entirely.

The honest limit. This is the plan we'd start an AI SaaS founder on, and it comes with no guarantee. Treat the schedule as a first draft: keep the formats your buyers save and DM you about, and drop the rest. Every company, name and number in the worked examples is made up to show the shape of the post.

The plan in one look

There are five formats spread over the funnel. The top gets you found by the right people, the middle earns their trust, and the bottom asks for the call once a month.

The five formats

01 Top of funnel

The teardown

What it is. Pick something from your buyer's world (a cold email, a sales call, a dashboard, a contract or a landing page) and take it apart in public: what's wrong with it, and how you'd fix it.

Why it works for AI SaaS. Buyers have watched too many polished AI demos to trust another one. A teardown applies your product's thinking to a problem they recognise, so they watch you do the job long before they see a login screen.

Template

I took apart [a real thing from your buyer's world] that [who sent or made it] [what happened to it].

[Number] things wrong with it:

1. [Problem]. [Why it costs them]. [The fix in one line].
2. [Problem]. [Why it costs them]. [The fix].
3. [Problem]. [Why it costs them]. [The fix].

The rewritten version: [the fixed thing, or a screenshot of it].

[One line on the pattern behind all three mistakes.]

Worked example · Acme Notes, a made-up AI note-taker for sales teams

A rep sent me the recording of a discovery call he lost. Thirty minutes, friendly, no next step. I went through the transcript line by line.

1. He asked about budget in minute four. The buyer hadn't admitted a problem yet, so budget was a stranger asking for money.
2. The buyer said "we tried something like this last year". He didn't ask what happened. That was the whole deal.
3. He ended with "I'll send over some info". No date, no owner, no reason to reply.

Below is the same call with three questions changed. The pattern behind all three: he was so busy running his script that he missed the moment the buyer handed him the deal.

The mistake that kills it: tearing apart a real person's work without permission. Anonymise everything, or ask first, or tear down your own old work. A teardown that humiliates someone makes you look cruel, and your buyer imagines it's them next.

02 Top of funnel

The insider stat

What it is. A pattern pulled from everything your product has seen across demos and calls, one finding per post, with the number and what it means for the reader.

Why it works for AI SaaS. You see more of your buyer's work than they do. A product that touches thousands of emails, calls or tickets is sitting on a research report nobody else can write, and publishing it makes you the person who knows how the job really gets done.

Template

We looked at [number] [calls / emails / tickets / demos] from the last [time period].

[The surprising finding, in one sentence, with the number.]

Why it happens: [your explanation in two or three lines].

What the best [teams / reps / founders] do differently: [the behaviour].

[One thing the reader can try this week.]

Worked example · Acme Notes (made up, numbers invented for illustration)

We looked at 2,000 discovery calls our customers ran last quarter.

The calls that turned into a second meeting had one thing in common: the buyer talked about a past failed attempt before the halfway mark.

Why it happens: when a buyer tells you what didn't work, they're telling you what they'll pay to avoid. Reps who ask "what have you tried?" early get that story. Reps who pitch early never hear it.

Try it this week: make "what have you already tried?" your second question.

The mistake that kills it: making the number up, or rounding it into something it isn't. Use only data you're allowed to share, say where it came from and how much of it there was, and never publish anything that identifies a customer. Get caught with one fake stat and readers will doubt every real one after it.

03 Middle of funnel

The AI opinion

What it is. A clear position on where AI belongs in your buyer's job and what you refuse to automate, one opinion per post, argued properly.

Why it works for AI SaaS. Most AI vendors claim they can do everything. When a founder says "we won't build that, and here's why", buyers hear someone who understands the job well enough to know where the machine should stop.

Template

We will never automate [a specific part of your buyer's job].

Everyone's building it. Here's why we're not: [your reason, in two or three lines].

What AI should do instead: [the part of the job it's genuinely good at].

The line I'd draw: [your rule, in one sentence they can repeat].

Where would you draw it?

Worked example · Acme Notes (made up)

We will never let AI send the follow-up email after a sales call.

Everyone's building it. Here's why we won't: the follow-up is the first thing the buyer reads from you without you in the room. If it sounds like every other AI email, you just told them you weren't really listening.

What AI should do instead: pull the three things the buyer actually said, word for word, so the rep writes a better email in two minutes.

The line I'd draw: AI remembers, the rep speaks.

The mistake that kills it: fake controversy, like "cold calling is dead", which is bait dressed up as a view. A real opinion costs you something, such as a feature you won't build or a customer you'd turn away, and if nobody could disagree with yours, keep thinking.

04 Middle of funnel

The objection crusher

What it is. One sales objection per post, answered in public: take the thing buyers say on calls and work through it properly.

Why it works for AI SaaS. AI buyers turn up with the same worries about data security, accuracy, "my team won't use it" and "we'll build it ourselves". Answer them on LinkedIn and people show up to the call half convinced, and your sales team gets a link to send the next time the objection comes up.

Template

"[The objection, in the buyer's exact words]."

I hear this on [how often] of our calls, and it's a fair worry.

[Where the worry is right.]
[Where it's wrong, with specifics.]
[What you do about the part that's right.]

If that's what's stopping you, [the one thing they can check or ask for].

Worked example · Acme Notes (made up)

"Our reps won't use another tool."

I hear this on most first calls, and it's a fair worry.

You're right that reps ignore tools that add work. That's most sales software.
Acme Notes doesn't add a step. It joins the calls already on their calendar and the notes land in the CRM before they've hung up.
The part you're right about: some reps will still ignore the summaries. So we show the manager who's opening them, and you can coach the rest.

If that's what's stopping you, ask for two weeks on one team and check the CRM yourself.

The mistake that kills it: treating the objection as stupid. Sneer at the worry and you sneer at every buyer who has it, so start with where they're right; owning the limit is what makes the rest of the answer believable.

05 Bottom of funnel

The direct offer

What it is. Once a month, a plain post that asks for the call: who it's for, who should skip it, what they get, and as much proof as you have permission to use.

Why it works for AI SaaS. After three weeks of teardowns, stats, opinions and objections, the right people already trust you, and plenty of them are waiting for a reason to get in touch. The offer gives them one. Naming who it isn't for makes the right buyer feel chosen.

Template

[Number] spots open in [month] for [what you offer].

It's for you if: [buyer, stage, situation]. [A second signal they'd recognise].

It's not for you if: [the buyer you'd turn away]. [A second disqualifier].

What you get: [deliverable 1]. [Deliverable 2]. [Deliverable 3].

Proof: [a customer result with permission]. [A second result]. [A short quote].

[How to ask: comment, DM, or a link to book.]

Worked example · Acme Notes (made up)

Five teams can join our pilot in March.

It's for you if you run a sales team of 10 to 50 reps and your CRM notes are a mess.

It's not for you if your team sells by email only. We're built for calls.

What you get: two weeks of setup with us, the CRM integration done for you, and a weekly call review with your manager.

Proof: [customer result with permission]. [Second customer result]. [Customer quote].

DM me "pilot" and I'll send the details.

The mistake that kills it: posting the offer every week, or never. Weekly offers teach people to scroll past you, and never asking leaves warm buyers waiting, so post it once a month at the end of the cycle, when the other eleven posts have done their work.

The weekly posting schedule

Three posts a week for four weeks, twelve in total. Pick your weekdays and keep them fixed, since showing up predictably matters more than which day you choose; this plan uses Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday.

WeekTuesdayWednesdayThursday
Week 101 Teardown03 AI opinion02 Insider stat
Week 201 Teardown04 Objection crusher02 Insider stat
Week 301 Teardown03 AI opinion04 Objection crusher
Week 401 Teardown04 Objection crusher05 Direct offer

Over the month that's six top of funnel posts, five middle and one offer. Teardowns run every week because they show the product best, and the offer lands last, once the most people have seen the other eleven.

Three rules for the schedule
  1. Write the week's three posts the Friday before. Never write on the day.
  2. Spend 20 minutes after each post replying to every comment, because a buyer's comment is often where a DM starts.
  3. In month two, repeat the cycle and swap in more of whichever format got the most saves and DMs.

How to measure it

Impressions make a poor scoreboard. One motivational post we've seen hit 72,031 impressions and brought 7 followers and no signups, which is what reach looks like when the wrong people see it. Track these instead, in this order:

SignalWhy it mattersWhere to find it
SavesSomeone wants it later: the best sign it helped.Post analytics
Profile visits from buyersA buyer got curious enough to look you up.Profile viewers, by title
Comments from buyersCount only the ones from your target titles.By hand
DMsA conversation began; note which post began it.Your inbox
Calls bookedThe only number that pays for the time.Calendar or CRM
The weekly scorecard
  1. Every Friday, write down for each post: format, saves, profile visits from buyers, buyer comments, DMs.
  2. Add a "how did you hear about us?" question to your booking form and count the LinkedIn answers.
  3. After four weeks, rank the formats. Keep the top two, fix the bottom one, and run the cycle again.
Give it time: judge the plan after 90 days. One post tells you almost nothing, while three full cycles show you which formats your buyers keep coming back for.

The one page version

  1. Every post shows the product working on a problem your buyer already has, without the hype.
  2. Top of funnel: teardowns every week, insider stats every other week.
  3. Middle: opinions on where AI belongs, and objections answered in public.
  4. Bottom: one direct offer a month, at the end of the cycle, with proof.
  5. Three posts a week on fixed days. Write them the Friday before.
  6. Judge it on saves, buyer profile visits, DMs and calls booked, never on impressions.
